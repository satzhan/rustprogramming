# Final

You will solve a problem, write down how you solved it, and hand that record to two other people. They will do the same for you. Then you will stand up and compare how three people, the two you received and yourself, worked through different problems. The comparison is the part that matters most.

This isn't a report on your own solution. It's a comparison where your process counts for exactly as much as the two you received, no more.

---

## What you do

**1. Pick a problem that will actually stall you.**

Read the problem. Before writing anything, picture the whole solution start to finish. If you can see it clearly (the approach is obvious and you're just not sure yet how it plays out in code) put it back. That's not solving, that's typing. Pick one where you get maybe two steps in and then genuinely don't know what happens next.

You have time to browse. Use it to reject a few before you land on one.

**2. Solve it.**

Work it through four phases. Understand the problem. Plan an approach. Try the plan. Reflect on what happened. The phases will not stay in order. You will plan, start trying, discover you misunderstood, and go back. That is the normal shape of solving something. Let it loop.

When you get stuck, work the stuck-point first. Bring AI in only at the wall, not before. When you use it, write down where you were stuck and how the solution it gave you connected to that stuck-point. The connection is the thing you are learning to see.

Include concurrency somewhere. A couple of threads doing something alongside your solution is enough. It does not have to make the program faster. If threading bought you nothing, say so and say why. That is a real finding about the problem, not a failure.

**3. Write the report, then hand it in.**

Lay out all four phases so someone who was not there could rebuild your process without guessing at missing steps. Inside that, mark the turning points: the moments your understanding shifted, where you abandoned a plan and why, where AI came in, what the reflection connected.

A report that reads smoothly and cleanly has usually had its turning points sanded off. The messy moments are the ones worth keeping. Mark them.

Submit your report. In return, you'll receive two reports from classmates. Read them closely before you build your presentation.

**4. Present the comparison.**

You have ten minutes. Name your axis in one sentence. *Where did each of us first get stuck, and what does that reveal?* is one example, not the only option. Give each of the three processes, the two you received and your own, a single concrete detail tied to that axis. Don't summarize the whole problem, just the moment that matters.

Then spend most of your ten minutes on the actual comparison: what's alike, what's different, and why. Close on one idea you didn't have before you read the other two reports.

Don't narrate three solutions in sequence. Compare them.

---

## How it is graded

Two scores.

**The record.** Your own work, graded directly. Honest process including dead ends, a report faithful enough to reconstruct from. The first part of this rewards documented effort, not correctness. A wrong approach you can explain beats a right one you cannot.

**The meta-analysis.** Your presentation, graded on the quality of the comparison. Not whether you retold the reports accurately, but what you noticed about the differences and what you made of them, and whether your closing idea is actually something you learned from the other two, not something you already believed.

Concurrency is not graded on whether it was a good idea. It is graded on whether you reasoned about it.
